Introduction

Setting

  • Potential outcomes \((Y(0), Y(1))\)
  • Treatment \(D \in \{0,1\}\)
  • Observe \(Y = Y(D)\)
  • Covariates \(X\)
  • Assume conditional independence \((Y(0),Y(1)) \perp D | X\)
  • Assume overlap (positivity): \(0 < P(D=1|X) < 1\) almost surely

\[ \def\Er{{\mathrm{E}}} \def\En{{\mathbb{En}}} \def\cov{{\mathrm{Cov}}} \def\var{{\mathrm{Var}}} \def\R{{\mathbb{R}}} \newcommand\norm[1]{\left\lVert#1\right\rVert} \def\rank{{\mathrm{rank}}} \newcommand{\inpr}{ \overset{p^*_{\scriptscriptstyle n}}{\longrightarrow}} \def\inprob{{\,{\buildrel p \over \rightarrow}\,}} \def\indist{\,{\buildrel d \over \rightarrow}\,} \DeclareMathOperator*{\plim}{plim} \DeclareMathOperator*{\argmin}{argmin} \]

Why not regression?

  • Average treatment effect \[ ATE = \int \Er[Y|D=1,X=x] - \Er[Y|D=0,X=x] dP(x) \]
  • Regression gives the best linear approximation to \(\Er[Y|D,X]\), so why not just estimate a linear regression \[ Y_i = \hat{\alpha} D_i + X_i'\hat{\beta} + \hat{\epsilon}_i \] and then use \(\hat{\alpha}\) as an estimate of the ATE?

Why not regression?

  • Partial out (Frisch-Waugh-Lovell theorem) \[ \begin{align*} \hat{\alpha} = & \frac{\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n Y_i (D_i - X_i'(X'X)^{-1}X'D)} {\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n (D_i - X_i'(X'X)^{-1}X'D)^2} \\ \inprob & \Er\left[Y_i \underbrace{\frac{D_i - X_i'\pi}{\Er[(D_i - X_i'\pi)^2]}}_{\equiv \omega(D_i,X_i)}\right] \\ = & \Er\left[Y_{0,i} \omega(D_i,X_i)\right] + \Er\left[(Y_{1,i}-Y_{0,i}) \omega(D_i,X_i)D_i\right] \end{align*} \] where \(\pi = \argmin_{\tilde{\pi}} \Er[(D_i - X_i'\tilde{\pi})^2]\)

  • Note: \(\Er[\omega(D,X)] = 0\), \(\Er[D\omega(D,X)] = 1\)

  • \(\omega(D,X)\) is only orthogonal to linear functions of \(X\), so \(\Er[Y_{0,i}\omega(D_i,X_i)] \neq 0\) in general when \(\Er[Y(0)|X]\) is nonlinear

Why not regression?

  • \(\plim \hat{\alpha} = \Er\left[Y_{0,i} \omega(D_i,X_i)\right] + \Er\left[(Y_{1,i}-Y_{0,i}) \omega(D_i,X_i)D_i\right]\)

  • What can be in the range of \(\omega(D,X) = \frac{D - X'\pi}{\Er[(D_i - X_i'\pi)^2]}\)?

Matching

Matching

  • If not regression, then what? \[ ATE = \int \Er[Y|D=1,X=x] - \Er[Y|D=0,X=x] dP(x) \]

Plug-in estimator

  • Plug-in estimator: \[ \widehat{ATE} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \left(\hat{E}[Y|D=1,X=X_i] - \hat{E}[Y|D=0,X=X_i] \right) \] where \(\hat{E}[Y|D,X]\) is some flexible estimator for \(\Er[Y|D,X]\)
    • if \(X\) is discrete, \(\hat{E}\) can be conditional averages or equivalently, “saturated” regression
    • if \(X\) is continuous, \(\hat{E}\) can be some nonparametric regression estimator
    • Original approaches to this problem used nearest neighbor matching to estimate \(\hat{E}[Y|D,X]\)
  • Downside:
    • Difficult statistical properties — choice of tuning parameters, strong assumptions needed, failure of the bootstrap for nearest neighbors Abadie and Imbens (2008)

Propensity Score

  • Let \(p(X) = P(D=1|X)\)
  • Note: \[ \begin{align*} \Er[Y|X,D=1] - \Er[Y|X,D=0] = & E\left[\frac{Y D}{p(X)}|X \right] - E\left[\frac{Y(1-D)}{1-p(X)}|X \right] \\ = & E\left[ Y \frac{D - p(X)}{p(X)(1-p(X))} | X \right] \end{align*} \]

Propensity Score

  • so \[ ATE = \Er\left[ \frac{Y D}{p(X)} - \frac{Y(1-D)}{1-p(X)}\right] = \Er\left[ Y \frac{D - p(X)}{p(X)(1-p(X))} \right] \]

Inverse propensity weighting

  • Estimator \[ \widehat{ATE}^{IPW} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{Y_iD_i}{\hat{p}(X_i)} - \frac{Y_i(1-D_i)}{1-\hat{p}(X_i)} \] where \(\hat{p}(X)\) is some flexible estimator for \(P(D=1|X)\)

  • Downside:

    • Difficult statistical properties — choice of tuning parameters, strong assumptions needed

Doubly Robust Estimator

  • Combines plug-in and IPW estimators
  • Estimator \[ \begin{align*} \widehat{ATE}^{DR} = & \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \hat{E}[Y|D=1,X=X_i] - \hat{E}[Y|D=0,X=X_i] + \\ & + \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{D_i(Y_i - \hat{E}[Y|D=1,X=X_i])}{\hat{p}(X_i)} - \\ & - \frac{(1-D_i)(Y_i - \hat{E}[Y|D=0,X=X_i])} {1-\hat{p}(X_i)} \end{align*} \]

Doubly Robust Estimator

  • Doubly robust in that:
    • Consistent as long as either \(\hat{p}(X) \inprob p(X)\) or \(\hat{E}[Y|D,X] \inprob \Er[Y|D,X]\)
    • Insensitive to small changes in \(\hat{p}(X)\) or \(\hat{E}[Y|D,X]\)
  • Allows nicer statistical properties
    • Weaker assumptions needed
    • Uses cross-fitting (sample splitting) so overfitting in \(\hat{p}(X)\) or \(\hat{E}[Y|D,X]\) does not bias the ATE
    • Asymptotic distribution is the same as if \(p(X)\) and \(\Er[Y|D,X]\) were known

Software

  • Advice: use the doubly robust estimator with nonparametric estimates for \(\hat{E}[Y|D,X]\) and \(\hat{p}(X)\)
  • Recommended package:
    • doubleml

Propensity Score Matching

from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression, LogisticRegressionCV
from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsRegressor
import sklearn

def propensitymatching(D,Y,X,psmodel=LogisticRegressionCV(scoring='neg_log_loss'),neighbormodel=KNeighborsRegressor(n_neighbors=1,algorithm='auto',weights='uniform')):
    pfit = psmodel.fit(X,D)
    ps = pfit.predict_proba(X)[:,1]
    ey1 = neighbormodel.fit(ps[D==1].reshape(-1,1),Y[D==1])
    ey0 = sklearn.base.clone(neighbormodel).fit(ps[D==0].reshape(-1,1),Y[D==0])
    tex = ey1.predict(ps.reshape(-1,1)) - ey0.predict(ps.reshape(-1,1))
    ate = np.mean(tex)
    return(ate, tex,ps)

ate,tex,ps=propensitymatching(data_with_categ[D],data_with_categ[Y],data_with_categ[X])
print(ate)

def ipw(D,Y,X,psmodel=LogisticRegressionCV(scoring='neg_log_loss')):
    pfit = psmodel.fit(X,D)
    ps = pfit.predict_proba(X)[:,1]
    ate=np.mean(Y*(D - ps)/(ps*(1-ps)))

def robustate(D,Y,X,psmodel=LogisticRegressionCV(scoring='neg_log_loss'),ymodel=LassoCV(), cluster=None):
    pfit = psmodel.fit(X,D)
    ps = pfit.predict_proba(X)[:,1]
    ey1fit = ymodel.fit(X[D==1],Y[D==1])
    ey0fit = sklearn.base.clone(ymodel).fit(X[D==0],Y[D==0])
    ey1 = ey1fit.predict(X)
    ey0 = ey0fit.predict(X)
    ate_terms = ey1 - ey0 + D*(Y- ey1)/ps - (1-D)*(Y-ey0)/(1-ps)
    ate = np.mean(ate_terms)
    # check if cluster is None
    if cluster is None :
        ate_se = np.sqrt(np.var(ate_terms)/len(ate_terms))
    else :
        creg=smf.ols("y ~ 1", pd.DataFrame({"y" : ate_terms})).fit(cov_type="cluster", cov_kwds={'groups': cluster})
        ate_se = np.sqrt(creg.cov_params().iloc[0,0])

    return(ate, ate_se, ps, ey1,ey0)

ate,se,ps,ey1,ey0 = robustate(data_with_categ[D],data_with_categ[Y],data_with_categ[X],cluster=data_with_categ['schoolid'])

Summary of Growth Mindset Results

  • Unadjusted difference in means (\(\approx 0.47\)) overstates the effect due to positive selection into treatment (e.g., students with higher success expectations are more likely to be treated).
  • Adjusting for covariates via multivariate OLS (\(\approx 0.40\)), propensity score matching, IPW, and doubly robust / doubleml (\(\approx 0.38\)) yields slightly smaller, consistent estimates.
  • Clustering standard errors by schoolid accounts for within-school correlation in both OLS and doubleml.

Do Growth Mindset Interventions Work?

  • No broad consensus, though both sides agree the average effect across all students is very small (\(d \approx 0.04\text{--}0.05\)).
  • Skeptical view: Meta-analyses by Sisk et al. (2018) and Macnamara and Burgoyne (2023) find weak overall effects that become statistically indistinguishable from zero after adjusting for publication bias, study quality, and researcher incentives.
  • Heterogeneity / targeted view: Yeager et al. (2019) find modest gains among lower-achieving students (\(d \approx 0.11\)), and Burnette et al. (2023) report positive effects for targeted/at-risk groups with high-fidelity implementation (\(d \approx 0.14\) for achievement; \(d \approx 0.32\) for mental health).
  • Methodological debate: Tipton et al. (2023) argue that pooling heterogeneous studies into a single grand mean obscures real subgroup effects, whereas critics argue those subgroup effects do not survive strict bias adjustments.
